Nobody plans it this way. Going deep was the sensible thing to do and it paid — it still pays for everything on this page. Which makes one question worth sitting with: if that single skill were worth less in five years, what else would you have?

What AI changed, and what it didn't

For decades, going deep into one field was the safest move available. Knowledge was hard to reach, so being the person who held it was valuable in itself.

AI has not made specialists useless. What it has done is make everyone else's field cheap to reach. The knowledge you used to be paid for holding is now a question away — for you, and for anyone competing with you.

What is still hard is judgement — knowing which part of all that information matters in front of you, how it fits with everything else, and what to do when your situation is not the one in the manual. That is the part you cannot look up, and it is built by working on more than one kind of problem.

Then
Knowing something other people didn't was enough.
Now
What pays now is leveraging what one field already knows on a problem sitting in another.

You are probably already T-shaped, but not for the world.

The standard advice is to be T-shaped: deep in one thing, wide across others. Most specialists already have the width. It just came from the job — the teams you had to work with, the tools you were handed, the neighbouring functions you learned because a project needed them.

The one you have

T-shaped for your job

Deep in your specialism, and wide enough to hold your own with the software team, the supplier and the finance side. Every bit of that width was learned because the role needed it — so all of it rises and falls with the same industry, at the same time.

The one that protects you

T-shaped for the world

The same depth, but the width comes from fields with no connection to your work at all. Because nothing in it depends on your industry, none of it is repriced when your industry is.

Learning many things is the easy half

This is the distinction the whole idea rests on, and it's the one most people skip.

Collecting

Jack of all trades

Ten hobbies, ten shallow shelves, nothing touching anything else. Interesting at dinner, worth nothing at work, and the usual result of just doing more courses.

Connecting

Polymath

The same ten things, wired together — so a problem in your own field gets solved with something borrowed from one of the others. The wiring is the whole value.

How this actually works

Nothing here runs as a programme. You take what interests you, when it interests you.

01 Keep the depth you have Your specialism is the asset, not the problem — it is what gives you something real to bring when you step outside it. For a child, school is that depth. It carries on exactly as it is, nothing here competes with it, and none of it is homework.
02 Pick what interests you Courses are on unrelated subjects on purpose, and you take only the ones that pull you. For children that choice is the entire point: curiosity they chose themselves is the kind that lasts, and choosing is a habit in its own right.
03 Learn how to learn Every course is also a worked example of getting into an unfamiliar subject from nothing — where to start, what to skip, and how to find the few things that hold a subject up. That method is what you keep. It works on the next subject without us.
04 Put two unrelated things together Taking a method from one field and using it on a problem in another is the move that separates a polymath from someone with a lot of hobbies. It is taught here directly, across unrelated subjects, because left to chance it simply doesn't happen.
What we won't claim. Nobody becomes a polymath in three months or three courses, and anyone selling that is selling a certificate. It is a lifelong habit. What Unboxed does is instil the curiosity and hand over the method for learning anything — where that goes next is yours.
